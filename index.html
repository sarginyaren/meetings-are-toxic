According to the authors, meetings usually show up when an idea isn't clear enough yet. So a meeting is a symptom, not the disease itself. That's the diagnosis. The treatment isn't booking a meeting either. Simplify the idea and settle it briefly over email or chat. The goal is simple: avoid meetings.

[Time: ~75 sec. Don't read all seven reasons. Stress 1, 2 and 3 and move quickly through the rest. If you like, add your own bad-meeting story here (30 sec).] So why "toxic"? The book gives seven reasons; let me highlight three. One: they chop your day into small, disconnected pieces. In the gaps before and after an hour-long meeting, nobody can do deep work. Two: they run on abstract words. We talk about "I think it should be like this" when we have no real code or design in front of us. Three: very little information moves per minute. The same information could have been read in two minutes. The rest will sound familiar: someone hogs the time, the topic drifts, the agenda is vague, and nobody prepared. The net result, in the authors' words: the number one enemy of productivity. [Move to the dark box at the bottom right. Pause.] There's math too. Two people have a single communication path, three people have three, four people have six. Ten people? Forty-five. The formula is n times n minus one, divided by two. As a team grows, the coordination load explodes. The fix is small, independent teams. We do the same in software: reduce dependencies and keep modules independent.

Getting Real, Chapter 384 / 5
[Time: ~60 sec. Go through the three cards as if counting on your fingers.] What if we can't avoid a meeting? Three simple rules. One: set a 30-minute timer. When it goes off, the meeting is over, no debate. Two: invite as few people as possible, only those who actually shape the decision. Three: no clear agenda, no meeting. Meetings should be the exception, not the rule. Hold one only when an important issue needs input, approval or agreement. [Balance line:] Don't get me wrong, the book doesn't say "never hold a meeting." It says a meeting shouldn't be the default.

The big idea

Every minute you save from a meeting is a minute for real work.

1

Before sending an invite, ask: could a message solve this?

2

Politely decline invites with no agenda or no role for you.

3

If you must talk, talk over something real: code, a screen, a demo.
